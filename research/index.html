Research statement

Where the method
breaks down.

My M.Sc. research segmented retail customers by combining RFM features with K-means clustering. It worked — three interpretable segments, a clean elbow, a defensible story. But the interesting part came afterwards, when I looked at what the method had quietly assumed: that clusters are roughly spherical, comparable in variance, and separable by Euclidean distance in a log-transformed space.

Behavioural data is not obliging about any of that. Spend distributions are heavy-tailed, a handful of high-monetary customers drag centroids around, and the boundary between "occasional" and "lapsing" is genuinely continuous rather than a partition. K-means returns an answer regardless. The question I keep coming back to is how to tell the difference between a segmentation that reflects structure and one the algorithm imposed.

That interest pulls in two directions I would like to pursue formally: density- and model-based alternatives that admit outliers and soft membership, and interpretability — because a segmentation nobody can explain is not a finding, it is a colour chart.

An honest unknown is worth more than a confident wrong answer. I would rather characterise uncertainty than decorate over it.

M.Sc. Research Project Unsupervised learning 2024–25

Customer Segmentation using the RFM Model and K-Means

Department of Information Technology, St. Xavier's College, Mumbai (Autonomous) · with Sejal Gawade · submitted toward the M.Sc. Big Data Analytics

Question

Can raw transaction records be reduced to a customer segmentation that is both statistically defensible and specific enough to act on?

Method, as run

Transaction log→ Aggregate per customer→ RFM features→ Log transform→ Min–Max scaling→ K-means→ Elbow selection

Transactions were aggregated per CustomerID into three features — Recency (days since last purchase), Frequency (transaction count) and Monetary (total spend). The analysis was restricted to UK customers, who dominate the dataset; that reduces noise from thinly-represented regions, at the cost of generalisability. A log transform addressed the heavy right skew typical of transactional spend, and Min–Max scaling prevented Monetary from dominating the distance metric purely through its units.

I ran two segmentations deliberately. The first was a rule-based RFM quintile score — each customer scored 1–5 on each dimension, concatenated into codes like 555 (Top Customers) or 111 (Lost). The second was K-means on the transformed features. The first is interpretable by construction; the second is learned. Reading them against each other is what made the limitations visible.

Results

Three segments emerged and were readable without straining: at-risk customers (infrequent, low-spend, long since seen), potential loyalists (moderate on every dimension), and loyal customers (frequent, high-spend, recent). The rule-based RFM codes broadly agreed at the extremes — top and lost customers landed where you would expect — and disagreed in the middle, which is the part worth paying attention to.

What this does not establish

  • The elbow is a heuristic, not a criterion. K = 3 is where the curve bends, not where a statistic says the partition is best. No silhouette analysis or gap statistic was computed, so cluster validity is argued visually rather than measured.
  • K-means assumes what the data does not supply. It seeks roughly spherical, similar-variance clusters under Euclidean distance. Log-RFM space is neither isotropic nor cleanly separable — the boundary the plot shows is partly an artefact of forcing a hard partition onto a continuum.
  • Outliers pull centroids. A small number of very high-Monetary customers exert influence far out of proportion to their count, and K-means has no mechanism to set them aside.
  • Restricting to the UK trades noise for scope. It sharpens the analysis and narrows what can be claimed from it.
  • Agreement between the two segmentations is not validation. Both are built from the same three features; they share their blind spots.

Where I would take it

The obvious next step is to stop assuming a hard partition. Gaussian mixture models would give soft membership and let the middle of the distribution stay ambiguous instead of being adjudicated; DBSCAN would allow density-defined segments and, importantly, permit points to belong to nothing at all; hierarchical clustering would expose whether the three-way split is stable or an accident of K. Beyond method, the features themselves are thin — three aggregates discard the entire temporal structure of a purchase history, and I suspect sequence matters more than the RFM framing allows.

M.Sc. Coursework Project Supervised learning 2024

Predicting Customer Churn: a comparison of five classifiers

M.Sc. Big Data Analytics · 10,000 bank customers, 14 raw attributes

Question

Which model family actually earns its complexity on tabular behavioural data — and what drives the prediction once you look inside?

Method, as run

Categorical attributes were one-hot encoded (Geography) or binarised (Gender), and identifier columns dropped. Because several numeric features were visibly skewed, I examined log, square-root, Box–Cox and Yeo–Johnson transforms before settling the feature set. Five classifiers were then trained and evaluated on a held-out split: logistic regression as a baseline, then decision tree, gradient boosting, random forest and XGBoost. The random forest was tuned with randomised search over a 5-fold cross-validated grid.

Two things are worth drawing out. First, Age carries more signal than every financial attribute, which reframes churn here as a life-stage phenomenon rather than a purely economic one. Second, the tree ensembles separate the classes well — AUC above 0.93 — while logistic regression lands twelve points lower on accuracy, suggesting the decision boundary is genuinely non-linear rather than merely noisy.

What this does not establish

  • Tuning bought essentially nothing. Randomised search moved accuracy from 87.98% to 88.06% — eight hundredths of a percentage point, well inside run-to-run variance. Reported honestly, this is a null result, not an improvement.
  • The classes are imbalanced, so accuracy flatters every model. Churners are roughly a fifth of the sample; a model predicting "stays" for everyone would score near 80%. Recall and AUC are the numbers that carry information here, and recall tops out at 0.81 — around one churner in five is still missed.
  • Impurity-based importance is biased toward high-cardinality and continuous features. Age is continuous and Gender is binary, so the gap between them is partly an artefact of the measure. Permutation importance or SHAP would be the honest test, and I did not run either.
  • No calibration was assessed. The scores rank well, which is what AUC measures, but nothing here shows the predicted probabilities mean what they say — and any cost-sensitive threshold would depend on exactly that.

Where I would take it

I would replace impurity importance with permutation and SHAP analysis, assess probability calibration before trusting any threshold, and treat the imbalance directly — cost-sensitive learning or resampling — rather than reporting accuracy over it. The more interesting question underneath is whether the Age effect is a genuine life-stage signal or a proxy for something unmeasured, which needs data this dataset does not contain.

Independent investigation 2026

A negative result I chose to publish

Question

One fulfilment centre ran three times slower than every other in the network. Why?

In an independent diagnostic project over a simulated 150,000-search quick-commerce dataset, one dark store — DS009 — showed a 6.13% on-time delivery rate against a network average near 42%. I tested four explanations as a chained sequence of SQL queries: peak-hour load, delivery distance, order workload, and picking-and-packing time.

All four were ruled out. No remaining variable in the dataset accounted for the gap. I wrote it up as an unresolved operational anomaly flagged for on-site investigation, rather than selecting the most plausible-sounding cause and presenting it as a finding.

I include this because it is the closest thing in my applied work to a research instinct: the analysis that matters is often the one that refuses to conclude. The full investigation, with all 26 documented queries, is on GitHub.

What I want to work on

Three threads, each one traceable to a specific limitation I ran into rather than a topic I found attractive from the outside.

Clustering under violated assumptions

Density-based and mixture models for behavioural data that is heavy-tailed, continuous at the boundaries, and full of legitimate outliers. When should a method be permitted to assign nothing at all?

Came from: K-means forcing a hard partition on log-RFM space

Interpretability that survives scrutiny

Feature attribution that holds up under the biases of the measure itself — permutation and SHAP over impurity — and calibration, so a predicted probability means what it claims.

Came from: Age dominating an importance ranking I could not fully trust

Learning where the data cannot leave

I build audit tooling that must run entirely on-premises, because the documents are not permitted to leave the building. That constraint — useful models under strict data locality — is one I find genuinely interesting rather than merely inconvenient.
